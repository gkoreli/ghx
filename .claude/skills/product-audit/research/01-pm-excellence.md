# What It Means to Be an Excellent Product Manager — A Sourced Research Base

**Purpose:** rigorous, citable groundwork for a reusable "product audit" skill. Generic PM knowledge, verified against primary/authoritative sources. Where useful, a note on how a competency reads for a developer-tool / dev-infra / AI-agent-tooling product (context: `ghx`, a code-reconnaissance CLI sidecar for AI coding agents).

All URLs below were fetched and confirmed live during this research pass (July 2026). ## Abstract

Excellent product management is not one skill but a *balanced portfolio* of competencies that most individual PMs only partially cover — which is why the strongest treatments of the discipline (Ravi Mehta's Competency Model, Reforge's archetypes, Marty Cagan's empowered-team model) define excellence relationally: an excellent PM either personally covers the critical competencies for their context, or builds a team/trio that jointly covers them, and *knows which is which*. Across sources, five convergent ideas recur: (1) PMs are accountable for **outcomes**, not output — shipping is not the job, changing a customer or business metric is (Perri, Cagan, Cutler); (2) **product sense/judgment** is the ability to make good decisions under irreducible ambiguity, and it is trained through reps + reflection, not innate gift (Doshi, Rachitsky, Cagan); (3) strong PMs separate **discovery** (de-risking what to build) from **delivery** (building it well), and treat both as disciplines with their own techniques (Cagan, Torres); (4) frameworks (JTBD, North Star + inputs, Opportunity Solution Trees, RICE, Kano, Working Backwards) are not truth generators but **structured ways to force explicit reasoning and falsifiable bets**, and an auditor should look for the reasoning trace, not just the artifact; (5) the line between a good and a bad PM is less about IQ and more about **agency, ownership, and appetite for ambiguity** (Horowitz's 1998 essay and Doshi's "High Agency" say nearly the same thing, thirty years apart, in different vocabularies) — this is the trait most auditors underweight because it is harder to check off than "wrote a PRD."

## 1. Core PM Competency Models

- **Ravi Mehta's Product Competency Model** (built at TripAdvisor with colleagues, refined into "The Competency Toolkit") organizes PM work into **12 competencies across 4 categories**: *Product Execution* (feature specification, product delivery, product quality/QA), *Customer Insight* (fluency with data, voice of the customer, UX design), *Product Strategy* (business outcome ownership, product vision & roadmapping, strategic impact), and *Influencing People* (stakeholder management, team leadership, managing up). Mehta calls this "the closest thing to a mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive definition of product management" available, and states explicitly that "even peak product managers excel at only a handful of these competencies" — the differentiator is *knowing your gaps and building a team that covers them*, not personal mastery of all 12. — [ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-skills](https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-skills/) — authoritative because Mehta led product/growth at Tinder, Facebook, TripAdvisor, and co-built this model with practitioners for practitioners rather than as an academic taxonomy; also republished/endorsed by Reforge, a leading practitioner-education company — [reforge.com/blog/product-manager-skills](https://www.reforge.com/blog/product-manager-skills) (verified via proxy after a direct-fetch 403; content matches the primary source and lists the same 12 skills with brief operational definitions for each, e.g. Feature Specification = "gathering requirements, defining functionality, and establishing clear, actionable goals").
- Mehta's companion essay defines **four PM "shapes"/archetypes** along two axes — Product Builder vs. Product Architect (execution vs. vision) and Product Manager vs. Product Leader (IC vs. organizational influence): the **Project Manager** ("great at execution, but hasn't yet mastered the craft of creating products that people love"), the **People Manager** (rallies teams/stakeholders but under-invests in customer insight/strategy), the **Growth Hacker** (fast, analytical, test-driven), and the **Product Innovator** (strong vision, often weak on execution — "innovative PMs often suffer a weakness that is a mirror image of their strength"). — [ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-roles](https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-roles/) — useful for an audit skill because it gives a *diagnostic shape*, not just a skill checklist: an auditor can classify which shape a PM/team currently is, and check whether that shape matches what the product actually needs right now.
- **Marty Cagan / SVPG explicitly reject splitting "Product Manager" from "Product Owner."** Cagan argues the product-owner responsibilities (backlog administration, Agile ceremonies) are "just a very minor part" of the PM's real job, and that companies who split the roles typically get "very weak product and little innovation" because no one takes true ownership and the two roles develop mutual distrust. "Learning Scrum or Kanban doesn't teach a product manager how to actually lead a product." — [svpg.com/product-manager-vs-product-owner-revisited](https://www.svpg.com/product-manager-vs-product-owner-revisited/) (verified via proxy after 403; Cagan is the most cited living authority on tech-company product management, founder of Silicon Valley Product Group, author of *Inspired* and *Empowered*, advisor to hundreds of product orgs including Google, eBay, Netflix product teams). This matters for an audit skill targeting a CLI/dev-tool: it's a warning sign if "PM work" on a project has been reduced to backlog grooming with no one accountable for whether the tool actually helps its users.
- **Ben Horowitz's "Good Product Manager/Bad Product Manager"** (1998, Netscape; later widely republished by a16z) remains the founding document of the "PM is a leadership job without authority" framing still used today. Good PMs "operate from a strong basis of knowledge and confidence" about market/product/competition and "take full responsibility and measure themselves in terms of the success of the product"; bad PMs have "lots of excuses." Good PMs "crisply define the target, the 'what' (as opposed to the how)"; bad PMs "feel best about themselves when they figure out 'how'" and "put out fires all day." Good PMs "focus the team on revenue and customers" and take "written positions on important issues"; bad PMs obsess over matching competitor features. — [a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager](https://a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager/) — authoritative as the original canonical essay in the field (Horowitz is a16z co-founder, ex-Netscape/Opsware exec who wrote it from direct product-management experience); still the essay most senior PMs cite as formative, per Lenny Rachitsky's 2026 interview with Horowitz on its backstory.
- **Nielsen Norman Group's "Product Manager Archetype"** (research-based UX authority, article by Sara Paul, Feb 2023) frames PM competency along five practical dimensions useful for a *cross-functional collaborator's* audit lens: **Goals** (rapid experimentation, value-driven prioritization, team alignment, risk mitigation), **Strengths** (strategic thinking, communication, decisive action under complexity), **Challenges** (information overload, detail retention, allowing/overstepping design's territory), **Activities** (backlog management, data analysis, competitive research, documentation), **Skillset** (medium technical depth, strong business acumen, typically less design depth). — [nngroup.com/articles/product-manager-archetype](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/product-manager-archetype/) — authoritative because NN/g is the standard-bearer for research-grounded (not opinion-based) UX/product practice, founded by Jakob Nielsen and Don Norman; this source is valuable specifically because it's written *for designers evaluating PMs*, i.e. an outside/adjacent-discipline audit lens rather than a PM grading themselves.

**Auditor lens for this section:** Which of the 12 competencies does this PM/team visibly cover today, evidenced in artifacts (specs, roadmaps, data dashboards, stakeholder docs) rather than self-report? Which competency gaps are *acknowledged and covered by someone else* vs. silently unfilled? Is the "PM" role in this project actually doing PM work, or has it collapsed into backlog administration (the Cagan/Horowitz warning sign)?

## 2. Product Sense / Product Judgment

- **Shreyas Doshi** frames "product sense" as the umbrella label for a cluster of skills that jointly enable "correct decisions even when faced with considerable ambiguity" — decomposed into **Strong Empathy** (finding customer needs beyond what AI/data analysis surfaces), **Simulation Skills** (foreseeing future states via domain expertise), **Strategic Thinking** (choosing target segments/differentiators), **Great Taste** (picking the best option among many plausible ones), and **Creative Execution** (building something competitors structurally can't copy). His argument for why this matters increasingly: as AI commoditizes execution, "superior judgment applied to AI outputs" becomes "the only real long-term career moat for product people." — [shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/why-product-sense-is-the-only-product](https://shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/why-product-sense-is-the-only-product) — authoritative because Doshi (ex-Stripe, Twitter, Google, Yahoo VP/Director of Product) is widely regarded as the most-cited living voice specifically on product judgment as a discrete, trainable skill, and this essay is his most direct, current definition (2025/2026).
- Doshi's companion essay on developmental maturity argues exceptional builders succeed through **superior contextual judgment, not slogans**: "what works must be discovered in your specific situation, through practice, not adopted from theory" — average PMs who copy mantras like "move fast" fail because they lack the judgment to know *when* that mantra applies. He also names a specific failure mode: builders become "attached to everything you build, and shackled by it, regardless of your development process or framework" (an IKEA-effect blind spot), and mature PMs diagnose failures as *thinking failures*, not external circumstance. — [shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/the-product-builders-true-journey](https://shreyasdoshi.substack.com/p/the-product-builders-true-journey) — same authority rationale as above; this essay is specifically useful for an audit skill because it gives a concrete anti-pattern to check for (sunk-cost attachment to a shipped feature/architecture) rather than an abstract virtue.
- **Marty Cagan** treats "judgment" as the connective tissue of product discovery itself, not a separate soft skill: "product is very much about judgement" — specifically judgment about how severe a risk is, how much evidence is needed before committing engineering time, how large the work truly is, and how much documentation is warranted. He is explicit that judgment is *taught through experience and coaching*, not framework compliance: "an engineering tech lead, or a product designer, or a product manager that has experienced a broad range of product work will have already learned many of the lessons necessary," and for less experienced PMs "we depend on the person's manager to provide the necessary coaching." — [svpg.com/discovery-judgement](https://www.svpg.com/discovery-judgement/) (verified via proxy after 403) — same Cagan/SVPG authority as above; the practical payoff is a testable heuristic — *rapid, cheap validation for low-risk/low-consequence bets, rigorous live-data testing reserved for truly risky, high-consequence ones* — which an auditor can check a team actually applies rather than uniformly over- or under-testing everything.
- **Lenny Rachitsky's newsletter/podcast** treats product sense as *learnable, not innate*: recommended practices are deepening user empathy, watching real users interact with the product to sharpen intuition, reverse-engineering everyday products to see what makes them work, deliberately studying how strong product thinkers reason, and staying current on technology capability shifts. Crucially, it proposes a **self-check for whether product sense is actually improving**: track your hit rate on product assumptions over time (e.g., "you were right 2/10 times... now 8/10 times") and treat that trend, reflected on explicitly, as the real signal. — [lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-develop-product-sense](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-develop-product-sense) — authoritative as the largest-circulation practitioner-sourced PM publication (Lenny Rachitsky, ex-Airbnb PM), aggregating patterns across hundreds of interviewed senior PMs rather than one person's opinion; useful to an audit skill precisely because it operationalizes an otherwise-fuzzy trait into a trackable calibration metric.

**Auditor lens for this section:** Does this PM's reasoning show up as *falsifiable predictions later checked against outcomes* (the calibration test), or only as post-hoc justification? When they invoke a mantra/best-practice, can they say *why it applies here* and *when it wouldn't*? Is there a visible mechanism (retro, decision log, 1:1 coaching) for judgment to actually improve over time, or is each decision made from a blank slate?

## 3. Strategy vs. Execution vs. Discovery vs. Delivery

- **Melissa Perri's "build trap"**: organizations fall into it when they measure success by **output** (features shipped, velocity, release count) instead of **outcome** (the business/customer result those outputs were supposed to cause). "Companies that live and die by outputs often fall into the 'build trap,' cranking out features to meet their schedule rather than the customer's needs." Her prescribed fix is a **product-led organization**: (1) a PM role with the right responsibilities/structure, (2) a strategy that enables good day-to-day decisions, (3) a real discovery process (experimentation, not opinion) for deciding what to build, (4) organizational incentives that reward outcomes, not shipped output. — [melissaperri.com/book](https://melissaperri.com/book) — Perri is the author of *Escaping the Build Trap* (O'Reilly, 2018) and a widely retained strategic advisor to Fortune 500 and scale-up product orgs; this is her own canonical framing of the book's thesis, not a third-party summary.
- **Marty Cagan's "empowered product teams" vs. "feature teams"** is the execution-side counterpart to Perri's diagnosis. Empowered teams are staffed with competent, cross-functional people (PM, designer, engineers) and are "given a small number of significant problems to solve," "accountable to deliver business results (outcomes) rather than shipping features (output)." Feature teams, by contrast, are handed "a list of activities, or a list of features to build" and told "to do the work as fast as they can" — producing what Cagan calls "teams of mercenaries." The organizing purpose differs too: empowered teams exist "to serve the customers, in ways that meet the needs of the business," while feature-team orgs position technology simply "to serve the business." — [svpg.com/empowered-product-teams](https://www.svpg.com/empowered-product-teams/) (verified via proxy after 403) — this is the thesis of Cagan's second book *Empowered* (2020), aimed specifically at product leaders, and is the most widely cited modern formulation of outcome-accountability at the team level.
- Cagan's earlier book *Inspired* supplies the **discovery/delivery split** underlying both of the above: "In product discovery we address risks upfront to make sure what we build is viable for our business. In product delivery we build solutions." Discovery exists to make sure that "when we ask the engineers to build a production-quality product, it won't be a wasted effort" — the best teams "tackle the biggest risks first." — cross-referenced via multiple secondary summaries plus Cagan's own SVPG essays (see Four Big Risks below, §4) since *Inspired* itself is a paywalled/purchased book without a single canonical free URL; this claim is corroborated by the freely-accessible SVPG essays that restate the same framework, so it is graded as *well-sourced but via the author's own restated essays rather than the book's original page numbers*.
- **Teresa Torres's "Continuous Discovery Habits"** operationalizes discovery as a weekly habit for a **product trio** (PM + designer + engineer), not a phase-gate: "a structured and sustainable approach to continuous discovery" built from starting with a clear outcome, interviewing customers weekly to surface opportunities, and assumption-testing candidate solutions before committing engineering effort. Marty Cagan himself endorses it: "If you haven't had the good fortune to be coached by a strong leader or product coach, this book can fill that gap." — [producttalk.org/continuous-discovery-habits](https://www.producttalk.org/continuous-discovery-habits/) — Torres has coached hundreds of product teams and taught 7,000+ people through Product Talk Academy; the book (135,000+ copies sold per the page) is the most widely adopted operational playbook for continuous (vs. big-batch/waterfall) discovery in the 2020s.

**Auditor lens for this section:** Can the team point to a *recent* decision where discovery (evidence-gathering before build) visibly changed what got built, or does "discovery" only happen retroactively to justify a decision already made? Is the team's roadmap expressed as *problems/outcomes* or as a *feature list*? For a dev-tool/CLI: is there a distinct, non-performative discovery loop with actual users of the tool (e.g., agent-eval episodes, developer interviews) that precedes feature commitments, or does the roadmap track engineering convenience only?

## 4. Frameworks a Great PM Reasons With

- **Jobs to Be Done (Christensen)**: customers don't buy products, they "hire" them to do a job. Originating HBR article: Clayton Christensen (Kim B. Clark Professor, Harvard Business School), Scott Cook (Intuit co-founder), Taddy Hall (Cambridge Group/Nielsen), "Marketing Malpractice: The Cause and the Cure," *HBR*, Dec 2005 — opens with Ted Levitt's "people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole" and argues "over 90%" of new consumer products fail because marketers segment by product/demographics instead of by the job being hired for. — [hbr.org/2005/12/marketing-malpractice-the-cause-and-the-cure](https://hbr.org/2005/12/marketing-malpractice-the-cause-and-the-cure) — page confirmed to load; full text is subscriber-paywalled, so byline/abstract were verified but the complete argument was cross-checked against the follow-up 2016 HBR piece below. Christensen later refined the framework with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, and David S. Duncan in "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done," *HBR*, Sept 2016, opening with the finding that 84% of executives call innovation critical while 94% are dissatisfied with their own innovation performance — the article's thesis is that most innovation misses because teams profile *customers* instead of the *job*. — [hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done) — confirmed to load (also subscriber-paywalled beyond the abstract); both are cited because HBR + Christensen is the primary, peer-vetted origin of JTBD, as opposed to secondary blog retellings.
  - The canonical illustrative case (McDonald's milkshakes) is best told by Christensen's own institution: researchers found ~40% of milkshakes were bought by morning commuters who "faced a long, boring commute and needed something to keep that extra hand busy and to make the commute more interesting" — the milkshake was competing with bananas and bagels, not other milkshakes, once you understand the *job*. — [library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing) — Harvard Business School's own faculty-research publication about their professor, more authoritative than third-party retellings and freely accessible (unlike the original HBR article).
  - *Applied to dev tools:* the "job" a developer/AI agent hires a CLI recon tool for is rarely "run commands" — it's something like "build enough confidence in an unfamiliar codebase to make a safe change fast." Framing ghx-style tooling around that job (not around feature parity with `grep`/`ctags`) is the JTBD move.
- **North Star Metric + Input Metrics (Sean Ellis; Amplitude/John Cutler)**: Sean Ellis (coined "growth hacking"; drove growth at Dropbox, LogMeIn, Eventbrite, Lookout; co-author *Hacking Growth*) defines the NSM as "the single metric that best captures the core value that your product delivers to customers and is the key to driving sustainable growth" — it must reflect real customer value, be able to trend "up and to the right" indefinitely (unlike a bounded ratio such as 30-day retention), avoid perverse incentives (e.g., average revenue per customer can reward *dropping* low-value-but-profitable customers), and stay simple enough for the whole org to recall. Examples: Airbnb = nights booked, Facebook = DAU, Uber = weekly trips. — [medium.com/growthhackers/finding-your-north-star-metric](https://medium.com/growthhackers/finding-your-north-star-metric-fc1c1f71cbcb) — Ellis is the primary originator/popularizer of the term in a growth-team context, writing in his own voice.
  - **Amplitude/John Cutler** operationalize this into a full **North Star Framework**: the NSM plus 3–5 **input metrics** that the team can actually move day-to-day and that jointly explain movement in the NSM. Cutler's key discipline: "If you can move your North Star directly, it's probably not a good North Star. The goal of the North Star is to be one level out of reach" — it should provoke the question "why is that going up or down," forcing teams to reason about mechanism instead of gaming a single number. — [amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework](https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework) and [amplitude.com/blog/good-bad-north-star-metric](https://amplitude.com/blog/good-bad-north-star-metric) — Cutler ran hundreds of NSM workshops as Amplitude's product evangelist; a **bad** NSM is a vanity or lagging metric an auditor should flag on sight (ARR, DAU-as-vanity, downloads, page views, story points).
  - **Outcomes over outputs**, same Cutler/Amplitude lineage: outputs are "the tangible deliverables teams produce (features, code, story points completed)... regardless of whether it creates meaningful impact"; outcomes are the actual behavior/business change. Cutler names the trap by its common industry nickname, the "feature factory," and notes teams sometimes rationally choose to run one short-term "while explaining the bet and hypothesis" — the sin is running one *silently*, without naming the tradeoff. — [amplitude.com/blog/why-outcomes-over-outputs](https://amplitude.com/blog/why-outcomes-over-outputs).
- **Opportunity Solution Trees (Teresa Torres)**: a four-layer visual map — **Outcome** (root; scopes what counts as relevant), **Opportunity space** (customer needs/pains/desires that would drive the outcome if addressed), **Solutions** (candidate products/features per opportunity), **Assumption tests** (experiments validating a solution before full build). Torres's core diagnosis of the failure mode this tool fixes: "the most common pathology in product work: jumping straight from a goal to a feature list," skipping the mandatory middle layer of validated customer needs, and "overreacting to the most recent customer interview, sales conversation, or support ticket" instead of weighing the whole opportunity landscape. — [producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/) — same Torres authority as §3; this is the single most concrete, checkable artifact in the discovery literature — an auditor can ask to *see* the tree.
- **RICE prioritization** (Sean McBride, Intercom): score = **(Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort**. Reach = number of people affected in a defined period; Impact = per-person effect size on a 0.25×–3× scale (minimal→massive); Confidence = how much you trust your own reach/impact estimates (50/80/100%); Effort = person-months required. The framework's own stated purpose: "minimize personal biases in decision making" and let PMs "defend their priorities" with a shared, falsifiable formula rather than gut-feel or seniority. — [intercom.com/blog/rice-simple-prioritization-for-product-managers](https://www.intercom.com/blog/rice-simple-prioritization-for-product-managers/) — this is Intercom's own product blog describing a framework its own product team invented and uses, i.e. a primary source, not a third-party summary.
- **Kano Model** (Noriaki Kano, Nobuhiko Seraku, Fumio Takahashi, Shinichi Tsuji, "Attractive Quality and Must-Be Quality," *Journal of the Japanese Society for Quality Control*, 14(2), 1984, pp. 39–48): categorizes features by the *shape* of their satisfaction curve, not a linear "more is better" — **Must-be** (invisible when present, painful when absent), **One-dimensional/Performance** (satisfaction scales linearly with investment — the classic battleground of feature competition), **Attractive/Delighter** (unexpected, disproportionate satisfaction when present, no penalty when absent), plus later-added **Indifferent** (no effect either way — a cost-cutting candidate) and **Reverse** (some customers are actively harmed by "more"). — [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kano_model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kano_model) — cited as a **secondary but reliable** source because the 1984 Japanese-language original is not freely available online; Wikipedia's treatment here explicitly cites the original bibliographic reference, which I verified rather than inventing, and the categorical structure is consistent across every practitioner source checked in this research pass. Flagged per the sourcing discipline as the weakest link in an otherwise primary-source-heavy set.
- **Working Backwards / PR-FAQ** (Amazon; Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, both former Amazon VPs, co-authors of *Working Backwards*): teams draft a mock **press release** (headline, customer problem, solution, spokesperson quote, hypothetical customer quote, call-to-action — under one page) plus an **FAQ** (external, customer-facing questions; internal questions covering TAM, required capabilities, risk, competitive differentiation, regulatory exposure, unit economics) *before* committing engineering resources. Review happens in a **Narrative meeting**: document is read silently for 15–20 minutes, then debated for ~40, explicitly optimizing for "truth-seeking vs. selling." Amazon's stated rationale ties back to its Customer Obsession leadership principle: "Start with the customer and work backwards… In practice, it is much harder than it sounds." Kindle, Prime, and AWS were all built through this process. — [workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process](https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/) — authoritative because Bryar and Carr personally ran this process at Amazon for years before writing the book describing it; this is a first-hand operator account, not an outside analyst's reconstruction.

**Auditor lens for this section:** For any roadmap item, can the team state the *job* it serves in the customer's words (not the PM's feature language)? Is there one North Star + a small number of named input metrics, or a scoreboard of a dozen equally-weighted numbers nobody can recite? Does a prioritization decision have an explicit, falsifiable RICE-style estimate behind it, or only a stack-ranked gut call? Is there a written OST, PR/FAQ, or equivalent artifact that predates the build decision (evidence of real sequencing), or was it authored after the fact to rationalize a decision already shipped?

## 5. Outcomes vs. Output, and Detecting the "Build Trap"

- **Definitional split** (Perri, Cutler, Cagan — convergent across all three, independently arrived at): **output** = what a team produces and can count without knowing if it worked (features shipped, PRs merged, story points, releases); **outcome** = a change in customer or business behavior that the output was a bet on causing. Perri: companies "ship features rather than cultivate the value those features represent." Cutler: outputs are deliverables "regardless of whether it creates meaningful impact." — see §3 and §4 citations above (melissaperri.com/book; amplitude.com/blog/why-outcomes-over-outputs).
- **Detection heuristics an auditor can actually apply**, synthesized from the above sources (this bullet is *synthesis*, not a direct quote from any one source): (a) ask what metric moved because of the last 3 shipped things — if the honest answer is "we don't know" or "we don't track that," the team is in the build trap; (b) check whether the roadmap is expressed as a feature list or as named problems/outcomes (Cagan's empowered-team distinction, §3); (c) check whether "done" for the team means "merged/shipped" or means "validated against the intended outcome" — Cutler's NSM discipline (§4) gives the sharper version of this: if the team's headline metric is something they can move directly by just shipping (e.g., "features shipped"), it is definitionally not a good North Star and is very likely masking a build-trap culture.
- **Business-viability blind spot**: Cagan's "Four Big Risks" (§1/§4-adjacent) make explicit that even outcome-oriented teams can miss a full risk assessment if they only check *value* (will users want it) without checking *viability* (does it work for sales, legal, support, unit economics, brand) — "whether this solution also works for the various aspects of our business," covering go-to-market fit, contractual/legal constraints, cost-effective acquisition, monetization, and brand consistency. Ownership: PM owns value + viability risk and is "accountable for the product's outcomes"; the designer owns usability risk and is "accountable for the product's experience"; the lead engineer owns feasibility risk and is "accountable for the product's delivery." — [svpg.com/four-big-risks](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/) (verified via proxy after 403).

**Auditor lens for this section:** Pull the last quarter's shipped list. For each item, is there a named metric it was supposed to move, and did anyone check afterward whether it moved? Is "done" defined as shipped or as validated? Does the team's stated headline metric fail Cutler's "can you move it directly" test? For a CLI/dev-tool specifically: is a new command/flag justified by "an agent needed this to complete task X faster/more reliably" (outcome) or only by "it seemed useful" (output)?

## 6. Signals of PM Excellence vs. Mediocrity

- **High Agency (Shreyas Doshi)**: the capacity to "find a way to get what you want, without waiting for conditions to be perfect or otherwise blaming the circumstances" — high-agency people either push through adversity or actively reshape it. Doshi's 2×2 (talent × agency): **Game Changers** (high talent + high agency — rare, outsized impact), **Frustrated Geniuses** (high talent + low agency — capitulate to "the system," produce a hit or two and stall), **Go-Getters** (lower talent + high agency — improve via initiative), and low-talent/low-agency performers with minimal impact. Traits: **ownership mindset** ("perhaps the most important"), proactivity, resilience. Skills: **creative execution** ("the most elusive skill"). A concrete diagnostic he offers: notice your own language — low-agency framing blames external systems, high-agency framing focuses on controllable levers. — [linkedin.com/pulse/high-agency-its-importance-how-cultivate-shreyas-doshi](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/high-agency-its-importance-how-cultivate-shreyas-doshi) (verified via proxy) — same Doshi authority as §2; notably, this restates Ben Horowitz's 1998 "good PMs take full responsibility... bad PMs have lots of excuses" almost point-for-point (§1), suggesting this is a durable, cross-generational signal rather than a fad.
- **Pre-PMF vs. post-PMF differences**: multiple secondary sources (podcast descriptions, newsletter summaries) attribute to Doshi a distinction between **Craftsperson**, **Operator**, and **Visionary** product-leader archetypes, with the Craftsperson framed as "a reasonable default for early-stage startups, especially those pre- or post-product market fit" because of strength in "translating ambiguous goals into concrete products," while Operators are better suited to scaling a proven product. **This claim is flagged as weakly sourced**: I was unable to retrieve a full transcript or primary essay text (the *20VC/Deciphr* podcast page returned only an episode outline, no transcript quotes) — treat the archetype *names* as reasonably confirmed (they recur consistently across multiple independent write-ups) but treat the *detailed mapping to PMF stage* as a secondary-source characterization pending a primary citation. — [deciphr.ai/podcast/20-product-shreyas-doshi...](https://www.deciphr.ai/podcast/20-product-shreyas-doshi-on-the-three-different-types-of-product-leaders-and-how-to-hire-them-the-6-different-product-metrics-you-need-to-know-and-what-good-is-for-each-of-them--table-stakes-features-vs-wow-features-what-to-prioritise) — included despite the caveat because the underlying intuition (different stages need different PM strengths) is independently corroborated by Cagan and Mehta's archetype work above.
- **The 30-year continuity check** (synthesis): Horowitz (1998) and Doshi (2020s) describe the same excellence/mediocrity boundary in different vocabulary — ownership over excuse-making, strategic "what" over tactical "how," judgment over process-compliance. An auditor should treat convergent, independently-arrived-at signals like this as higher-confidence than any single framework, precisely because they were not copied from each other.

**Auditor lens for this section:** When something goes wrong on this PM's watch, does their own account center controllable decisions they made, or external blockers (eng capacity, unclear specs from above, "the system")? When facing an ambiguous, high-stakes call, do they visibly gather the minimum necessary evidence and *decide*, or do they escalate/defer indefinitely? Is there a track record of them changing the "how" without waiting for someone else to unblock the "what"?

## 7. How Excellence Reads for a Developer-Tool / Dev-Infra / AI-Agent-Tooling Product (synthesis)

This section is **explicitly Fable/orchestrator synthesis**, not a direct claim from any cited source — offered because the requesting context (`ghx`) is dev-infra/agent-tooling and none of the sources above write specifically about that niche.

- The "customer" for a tool like `ghx` is not a single human end user but potentially a *dyad*: the AI agent consuming the tool's output programmatically, and the human developer who trusts (or doesn't) the agent's resulting actions. JTBD analysis (§4) should be done for both — the job an agent "hires" the tool for (fast, low-token-cost codebase orientation) may differ from the job the human overseeing the agent needs done (confidence that the agent won't hallucinate or break something).
- North Star + input metrics (§4) for an eval-harness-equipped agent tool are unusually tractable compared to most software categories: episode-level task success, judge-scored quality, and token/time cost are all instrumentable, which is rare. The build-trap risk (§5) is therefore not "we can't measure outcomes" but "we measure the wrong outcome" (e.g., optimizing for judge score without checking judge calibration — directly analogous to Cutler's vanity-metric warning).
- RICE and Kano (§4) apply cleanly to CLI surface decisions (new subcommands/flags): Reach = fraction of agent episodes that would invoke it; Kano status of a given capability (must-be vs. delighter) is discoverable from eval anomaly review rather than customer surveys, since agent behavior is directly observable.

## How This Feeds the Audit Skill

Candidate audit questions/checks, grouped by the section that grounds them. These are meant to be answerable from artifacts (docs, roadmaps, commit history, metrics dashboards, decision logs) rather than from self-report, per the Evidence Contract discipline this project already follows for other audits.

**Competency coverage (§1)**
1. Map current PM/team activity against Mehta's 12 competencies (execution/insight/strategy/influence). Which are visibly covered by artifacts? Which are silently missing, and does anyone know they're missing?
2. Is there a single owner accountable for both "outward" (customer/business) and "inward" (backlog/delivery) responsibility, or has the role split into a Cagan-style PM/PO gap with no one truly owning outcomes?
3. Pull 3 recent "PM-shaped" decisions. Do they read as Horowitz's "good PM" pattern (crisp what, owned outcome, no excuses) or "bad PM" pattern (fire-fighting how, competitor-feature chasing, blame-shifting)?

**Product sense / judgment (§2)**
4. Find 3–5 past predictions/bets this PM made (a launch will move metric X, a design will reduce support tickets, etc.). Were they tracked against actual outcomes? Is there a rising hit-rate trend, or no tracking at all?
5. When a "best practice" or mantra is invoked in a doc/PR/decision, is the *why it applies here* stated, or is it cargo-culted?
6. Is there a coaching/retro mechanism through which judgment calls get reviewed and improved, or does every decision start from zero?

**Strategy/execution/discovery/delivery split (§3)**
7. Is the roadmap expressed as named problems/outcomes, or as a feature backlog? (Cagan empowered vs. feature-team test.)
8. Find the most recent nontrivial build decision. Does discovery evidence predate it (interviews, experiments, a written OST) or only follow it (retroactive justification)?
9. Is there a recurring (not one-off) discovery cadence involving real users/agents, or was discovery a one-time phase at project inception that's now stale?

**Frameworks in active use (§4)**
10. For the top 3 roadmap items, can the team state the customer/agent "job" being served, in the customer's language, not feature language?
11. Is there one North Star metric plus a small, named set of input metrics — or a sprawling, equally-weighted dashboard nobody can recite from memory? Does the North Star pass Cutler's "can the team move it directly" test (if yes, it's a bad NSM)?
12. Are prioritization calls backed by an explicit, falsifiable estimate (RICE-style: reach/impact/confidence/effort), or only by seniority/gut stack-ranking?
13. For a major initiative, is there a PR/FAQ-equivalent artifact (working-backwards narrative) written *before* commitment, or does the team jump straight from goal to build?

**Outcomes vs. output / build-trap detection (§5)**
14. For the last quarter's shipped items: was a target metric named in advance for each, and was it checked afterward? What fraction had neither?
15. Does "done" mean shipped/merged, or validated-against-intended-outcome?
16. Run Cagan's Four Big Risks checklist against the current roadmap: is business-viability risk (legal, support cost, monetization, brand) being assessed at all, or only value/usability/feasibility?

**Excellence signals / agency (§6)**
17. When something recently went wrong, does the PM/team's own retrospective account center controllable decisions or external blame ("the system," under-resourcing, unclear specs from above)?
18. Facing the last ambiguous, high-stakes call: did the team gather minimum sufficient evidence and decide, or defer/escalate indefinitely?
19. Is there evidence of the team changing "how" without waiting to be unblocked on "what" (a concrete high-agency behavior), or a pattern of stalling until conditions are perfect?

**Dev-tool/agent-tooling specific (§7, synthesis — use cautiously, not directly sourced)**
20. Is the "customer" job defined separately for the consuming AI agent (token cost, task success) and for the human overseeing it (trust, safety, explainability)? Are both tracked, or only one?
21. Given this project already has an eval harness, is the North Star metric something the team could move by directly optimizing the harness/judge (bad NSM, Goodhart risk) or does it require a real behavior change to move?
22. Is Kano-style categorization (must-be vs. delighter vs. indifferent) for CLI surface decisions grounded in observed agent-episode behavior, or asserted from PM intuition alone?
