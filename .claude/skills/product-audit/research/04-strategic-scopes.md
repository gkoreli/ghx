# Strategic Scopes for a Product Audit — What Are We Even Auditing For

## Abstract

A product audit that only checks code quality, roadmap hygiene, or execution velocity answers "are we building it right?" It never answers "are we building the right thing, for the right market, in a way that can't be trivially copied?" This document catalogues nine strategic **scopes** — orthogonal axes a rigorous audit must interrogate — each with its authoritative framework(s), the specific diagnostic questions the framework forces, and the failure modes practitioners fall into when they skip or fake the scope. The nine scopes are: (1) product-market fit, (2) competitive analysis, (3) industry/market analysis, (4) positioning & differentiation, (5) north-star/strategy alignment, (6) gap analysis, (7) adjacent-opportunity expansion, (8) business model/monetization, and (9) moat/defensibility. Frameworks are generic and transferable to any product; where useful, a short note applies the framework to a developer-tool / AI-agent-infrastructure / open-source-adjacent product — the shape of `ghx`, a Go/Cobra CLI positioned as a "code reconnaissance sidecar" inside an "Agent Sidecar Framework," competing/adjacent to codemap, repomap, Sourcegraph/Cody, Aider, CodeRabbit, GitHub code search, and ast-grep, whose stated moat thesis is "the agentic brain, not the tools." Those notes are clearly separated from the generic framework content and are illustrative, not prescriptive — this document does not audit `ghx` itself.

All numbered claims below are traceable to a specific, deep-linked source with a stated rationale for its authority. Where a primary source could not be fetched directly (paywall, blocked fetch, binary PDF), that is disclosed rather than silently smoothed over. A full accounting is in the **Source Ledger** at the end.

## 1. Product-Market Fit

**Core question:** *Is there a real, measurable population of customers who would be seriously hurt if this product disappeared — or are we still searching?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Marc Andreessen / Andy Rachleff, "The Only Thing That Matters"** — [pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html](https://pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html). Andreessen's 2007 essay coined "product/market fit" for a mass audience and popularized what he attributes to Benchmark co-founder Andy Rachleff's "Corollary of Startup Success." *Rationale:* this is the term's origin essay — every later PMF framework (Ellis, Vohra, a16z's own retrospectives) treats it as the canonical reference point. Verified by direct fetch: the essay defines PMF as "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market," asserts "the only thing that matters is getting to product/market fit," and gives qualitative before/after signals (pre-PMF: customers don't get value, word of mouth doesn't spread, sales cycles drag; post-PMF: customers buy as fast as you can make it, usage grows as fast as you can add servers, press calls you).
- **Sean Ellis, the 40% "very disappointed" test** — [medium.com/growthhackers/using-product-market-fit-to-drive-sustainable-growth-58e9124ee8db](https://medium.com/growthhackers/using-product-market-fit-to-drive-sustainable-growth-58e9124ee8db). Ellis (who coined "growth hacking") ran this single-question survey — "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" (Very / Somewhat / Not disappointed) — across hundreds of startups and found the ones that scaled almost always cleared 40% "very disappointed." *Rationale:* this is the first person to turn PMF from a feeling into a measured, comparable number, in his own words.
- **Rahul Vohra (Superhuman), "How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit"** — [review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/). *Rationale:* the most rigorous public operationalization of Ellis's test — verified by fetch, it adds a segmentation step (Very Disappointed → build a "High-Expectation Customer" profile from what they love; Somewhat Disappointed whose stated main benefit matches your core value prop → convertible, prioritize their blockers; Not Disappointed → deliberately ignore) and a repeatable four-step loop (Segment → Analyze → Build 50/50 between amplifying VD love and fixing SD friction → Repeat), moving PMF score from 22% to 58% in three quarters.

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- What is the current Sean Ellis score, measured on active users (not signups), and is it trending up or down release over release?
- Who exactly is in the "very disappointed" cohort — can we name their shared job, workflow, or persona, or is it a diffuse grab-bag?
- Retention cohort curves: do they flatten (fit) or keep decaying toward zero (no fit)? PMF is a retention-curve claim as much as a survey claim.
- Is qualitative "market pull" visible — inbound demand, unprompted word of mouth, users self-organizing support/tips content — or is all growth outbound-pushed?
- For the "somewhat disappointed" segment: what's the modal reason for the tepid rating, and is it a roadmap item or a segment-fit problem (wrong customer, not wrong feature)?
- Has PMF been *re-measured* since the last major pivot in ICP, pricing, or core workflow, or is the team running on a stale, pre-change signal?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Vanity substitution:** citing total signups, GitHub stars, or download counts as PMF evidence instead of retention or the disappointment survey — none of these distinguish curiosity from dependence.
- **Averaging away the signal:** running the Ellis survey against the whole user base instead of *active* users, diluting the score below the 40% threshold artificially (or inflating it if surveying only power users).
- **Feature-chasing the "not disappointed" cohort:** per Vohra, this group is definitionally not convertible with more features; teams that build for them anyway are optimizing for the wrong segment.
- **Treating PMF as a one-time gate** rather than a metric that can be lost — market shifts, new entrants, or pricing changes can erode a previously-real fit.

## 2. Competitive Analysis

**Core question:** *What structural forces determine who captures value in this market, and where does this specific product sit relative to the alternatives customers actually consider?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Michael Porter, "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy"** — primary summary at Porter's own research institute: [isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx); original 1979 HBR article and its 2008 update at [hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy](https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy). *Rationale:* the foundational structural framework for industry analysis — verified by fetch of Porter's own institute page, which states the five forces (threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, rivalry among existing competitors) "determine the competitive structure of an industry, and its profitability," and that "industry structure, together with a company's relative position within the industry, are the two basic drivers of company profitability." This is deliberately an *industry-economics* lens, distinct from a feature-by-feature teardown.
- **April Dunford, competitive-alternatives step of the positioning framework** — see full citation in Scope 4; the relevant piece here is that Dunford makes "list your true competitive alternatives" (what would the customer do if you *didn't exist* — including do-nothing and manual workarounds, not just named competitors) the mandatory first move before any feature comparison, because it defines the frame the buyer is actually judging you against.
- **Structured competitive teardown practice** (feature/positioning matrices, win-loss analysis): synthesized from current practitioner methodology — a teardown separates Product, Pricing, Positioning, Traction, and messaging into independent axes rather than a single "who has more checkboxes" table, and win-loss interviews (asking *closed-lost and closed-won* customers why, in their own words) are the antidote to guessing at differentiation from the outside. *Labeled synthesis — no single named primary author; this is now common product-marketing practice, not one person's proprietary IP.*

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Who are the *true* competitive alternatives, including "do nothing," "build it in-house," "use a spreadsheet/script," and adjacent tools repurposed for the job — not just the obvious named rivals?
- Applying Five Forces: how much power do suppliers (in a dev-tool context: model providers, the host IDE/agent runtime) have to squeeze margin or capability? How much power do buyers (individual developers vs. enterprise platform teams) have to walk? How real is the substitute threat (a competitor's adjacent feature that does 80% of the job)? How low are entry barriers (can a solo developer clone the core loop in a weekend)?
- Win/loss: in the last N closed-lost and closed-won evaluations, what was the *stated* deciding factor, and does it match what the team believes the differentiator is?
- Where does the product win on structural axes (integration depth, data advantage, workflow lock-in) versus surface-level feature count — and is the team competing on the axis it can actually defend?
- Rivalry intensity: is the category currently commoditizing (multiple credible open-source or big-platform entrants converging on the same feature set), and if so, what's the plan for a category where the median feature becomes free?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns — including the feature-parity trap

- **The feature-parity trap:** treating "close every checkbox gap versus Competitor X" as the strategy. Once a category is well-defined, multiple vendors can plausibly ship the same feature list at similar price — matching checkboxes doesn't create separation, it just proves the category is converging. Feature-matching is often a symptom that positioning (Scope 4), not the roadmap, is the actual unsolved problem.
- **Competing only on the visible incumbents** while ignoring "do nothing" / in-house scripts as the modal alternative — for infra and dev-tool categories in particular, the true competitor is frequently "the team's own bash script," not the funded startup.
- **One-time competitive research** — a Five Forces or teardown analysis done once at founding and never refreshed as the market commoditizes or new entrants (e.g., a foundation-model vendor shipping the feature natively) change the rivalry structure.
- **Confusing feature depth with structural advantage** — a longer feature list is not a moat (see Scope 9); competitive analysis that stops at "we have more features" hasn't actually engaged Porter's question of *who captures the value* over time.

## 3. Industry / Market Analysis

**Core question:** *How big is the addressable opportunity, how mature/commoditized is this part of the value chain, and why is now — not two years ago, not two years from now — the right time?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **TAM / SAM / SOM, rigorously grounded in Bill Aulet's "Disciplined Entrepreneurship" (MIT)** — [mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/disciplined-entrepreneurship-6-questions-startup-success](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/disciplined-entrepreneurship-6-questions-startup-success) (secondary summary of Aulet's book/course; the book is the primary source: Aulet, *Disciplined Entrepreneurship: 24 Steps to a Successful Startup*, Wiley, 2013). *Rationale:* Aulet's MIT methodology is the most rigorous, bottom-up (not top-down-guessed) approach to market sizing in wide practitioner use — it forces sizing from a narrow, winnable **beachhead market** up, rather than citing an industry-wide TAM and asserting 1% capture.
- **Bill Gurley, "How to Miss By a Mile: An Alternative Look at Uber's Potential Market Size"** — [abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/). *Rationale:* Gurley — Uber's board member and lead Benchmark investor, writing on his own long-running VC blog — is the definitive rebuttal to naive backward-looking TAM math, arguing directly against NYU professor Aswath Damodaran's TAM estimate that assumed Uber's market equaled the existing taxi/limo market: "When you materially improve an offering, and create new features, functions, experiences, price points, and even enable new use cases, you can materially expand the market in the process." This is the canonical caution that **TAM is a claim about a future world, not a lookup of an existing one** — essential for any product that expands or redefines its category rather than taking share in a static one.
- **Simon Wardley, Wardley Mapping — the Evolution Axis** — [blog.gardeviance.org/2016/08/exploring-map.html](https://blog.gardeviance.org/2016/08/exploring-map.html), the primary text of Wardley's freely-licensed (CC BY-SA) *Wardley Maps* book, published serially on his own blog. *Rationale:* Wardley Mapping is the only widely-adopted framework that plots a market not just by size but by where each component of the value chain sits on a maturity spectrum — Genesis → Custom-Built → Product/Rental → Commodity/Utility — verified by fetch: "the transitional domain is associated with reducing uncertainty, declining production costs, increasing volumes and highest profitability," while commodity-stage components are "commonplace, standardised and a cost of doing business." This directly answers "is the layer I'm building on/competing in about to commoditize out from under me?" Complementary primary text on organizing around evolutionary stage: [blog.gardeviance.org/2012/06/pioneers-settlers-and-town-planners.html](https://blog.gardeviance.org/2012/06/pioneers-settlers-and-town-planners.html) (Pioneers/Settlers/Town Planners).
- **Bill Gross, "The single biggest reason why start-ups succeed" (TED 2015)** — [ted.com/talks/bill_gross_the_single_biggest_reason_why_start_ups_succeed](https://www.ted.com/talks/bill_gross_the_single_biggest_reason_why_start_ups_succeed). *Rationale:* Gross, founder of startup studio Idealab, scored 100+ of his own portfolio companies on five factors (idea, team, business model, funding, timing) and found timing was the single largest determinant of outcome — the canonical "why now" data point, using the Z.com (too early, broadband too thin) vs. YouTube (right after codec + broadband thresholds cleared) contrast as evidence.
- **Theodore Levitt, "Exploit the Product Life Cycle"** — [hbr.org/1965/11/exploit-the-product-life-cycle](https://hbr.org/1965/11/exploit-the-product-life-cycle), HBR, Nov 1965. *Rationale:* the original article that turned the product life cycle (introduction → growth → maturity → decline) from a vague biology metaphor into an actionable strategic tool — the standard reference for "how mature is this category, and does our strategy match the stage" (e.g., a maturity-stage strategy of feature-differentiation is wrong for a genesis-stage category still fighting for a category definition).

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- What is the beachhead-market-up TAM (Aulet method: narrow winnable segment × realistic revenue-per-customer), not just a top-down "% of a huge existing market" guess?
- Following Gurley: does this product's TAM assume the *existing* category boundary, or does it credibly expand/redefine the category the way the underlying capability (e.g., a new model capability, a new protocol) unlocks new use cases the old market didn't have?
- Wardley-mapped: which components of the value chain this product depends on (or competes with) are still Genesis/Custom-Built (contestable, differentiating) versus already Commodity/Utility (a losing place to compete, a safe place to build *on top of*)?
- Why now: what specific enabling condition (cost curve, new protocol/standard, new distribution channel, regulatory shift, a foundation capability crossing a usability threshold) makes this the right window, and would the same product have failed 18 months ago for a specific, nameable reason?
- What life-cycle stage is the category in, and does the current go-to-market and roadmap strategy match that stage (genesis-stage category definition vs. maturity-stage share-taking)?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Top-down TAM theater:** "the global software market is $X trillion, if we get 1% that's $10B" — the single most common and least credible market-sizing move; Aulet's beachhead method and Gurley's essay both exist specifically to correct this.
- **Static TAM in a dynamic category:** sizing the market as it exists today when the product's own thesis is that it changes what's possible (classic Uber-vs-taxi-market error, in reverse — under-counting because the analyst used the old frame).
- **No Wardley-style value-chain view at all:** treating "the market" as a monolith instead of a stack of components at different evolutionary stages, which hides where genuine differentiation is even *possible* (you can't out-differentiate a commodity layer; you can only build fast on top of it).
- **"Why now" as an afterthought slide** instead of a falsifiable claim — a rigorous audit asks the team to name the specific threshold that was crossed, and treats "we just believe the timing is right" as a red flag, not an answer.

## 4. Positioning & Differentiation

**Core question:** *If a well-informed prospect fully understood this product, would they conclude "obviously, that's what I need" — or does the product require explanation to be understood as valuable at all?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome: How to Nail Product Positioning So Customers Get It, Buy It, Love It*** — primary source, author's own site: [aprildunford.com/books](https://www.aprildunford.com/books); detailed secondary walkthrough of the method (verified by fetch): [userlist.com/blog/positioning-overhaul/](https://userlist.com/blog/positioning-overhaul/). *Rationale:* Dunford, a working positioning consultant (ex-VP Product Marketing at multiple B2B companies), wrote the standard modern text on positioning as a discrete, buildable artifact rather than a vague "brand feeling." Her method's components, confirmed by fetch: (1) **competitive alternatives** — what would the customer do if you didn't exist; (2) **unique attributes** — what you have that alternatives don't; (3) **value** — what value those attributes enable, grouped into themes; (4) **target market characteristics** — who cares most about that value; (5) **market category** — the frame of reference that makes your strengths obvious (head-to-head vs. an existing category, a subcategory/niche, or a new category); plus a "layer on a trend, carefully" step and a one-page positioning canvas to keep the org aligned. *Positioning determines* messaging, not the reverse — Dunford's central warning, echoed in the summary, is that most companies write messaging before doing positioning and then wonder why the messaging doesn't land.
- **Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, Kevin Maney, *Play Bigger* — category design** — [playbigger.com/media/what-is-category-design-in-marketing-and-why-is-it-an-important-strategy](https://www.playbigger.com/media/what-is-category-design-in-marketing-and-why-is-it-an-important-strategy). *Rationale:* the standard reference for the more aggressive positioning move — not fitting into an existing category's frame (Dunford's default path) but defining a *new* category so the product is judged by criteria it invented and leads. The book's own research claim, cited across secondary sources, is that category-creating "category kings" capture the outsized majority of a market's value — a strong argument for treating category choice as a strategic decision, not a marketing afterthought.

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Competitive alternatives: has the team actually listed what customers do today in the *absence* of this product — including manual workarounds — or does positioning start from "we're better than [named competitor]" without grounding in the true alternative set?
- Unique attributes → value themes: for each claimed differentiator, is there a traceable "so what" to a value a specific customer segment cares about, or is it a feature listed because it was built, not because it's valued?
- Market category: is the product asking to be judged inside an existing, well-understood category (fastest path to comprehension, but caps upside at that category's perceived value) or defining a new one (higher payoff, much higher cost and risk of the category never being adopted by the market's vocabulary)? Is that a deliberate choice or an accident of how the product was described in its first pitch deck?
- Target market: who specifically "cares a lot" about the value themes — is that segment named precisely enough to prioritize against, or is the target market still "developers" / "companies"?
- Positioning-to-messaging traceability: can any piece of external messaging (landing page, README, pitch) be traced back to one of the five positioning components, or is messaging free-floating and inconsistent across surfaces?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Messaging without positioning:** polishing taglines and landing-page copy while the underlying positioning (what category, versus what alternatives, for whom) was never explicitly decided — Dunford's most-repeated warning.
- **Default/accidental category:** ending up in an existing category by inertia (because a competitor used the term first, or because it was the easiest label at launch) rather than by comparing the payoff of "win inside an existing frame" versus "define a new frame."
- **Category creation without the payoff plan:** attempting category design (a high-cost, high-risk move per *Play Bigger*) without the sustained "point of view" content and market-education investment the category requires to take root — half-committing to a new category name is often worse than staying in an existing one.
- **Positioning frozen at launch:** never revisiting positioning as the competitive alternative set changes (new entrants redefine what "obviously" means) or as the product's own capability set outgrows its original frame.

## 5. North-Star / Strategy Alignment

**Core question:** *Can every meaningful item on the roadmap be traced, in one or two logical steps, back to the stated strategy — or is the roadmap a backlog of disconnected asks that happen to ship?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Melissa Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap*, and "What Is Good Product Strategy?"** — [melissaperri.com/blog/2016/07/14/what-is-good-product-strategy](https://melissaperri.com/blog/2016/07/14/what-is-good-product-strategy) (verified by fetch). *Rationale:* Perri's book is the standard practitioner text on the "build trap" — organizations that measure themselves by output (features shipped) instead of outcome (progress toward strategy) — and her strategy structure gives an auditable chain: **Vision** (long-term, qualitative destination) → **Challenge** (the one business objective tackled first on the way to vision) → **Target Condition** (measurable milestones breaking the challenge down) → **Current State** (the quantified baseline). Verified quote: "when we lock ourselves into planning to build a set of features (ehem, Roadmaps), we rarely stop to question if those features are the right things to build to reach our goals."
- **Amazon's Working Backwards / PR-FAQ process** — [workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/](https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/), documented by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr (former Amazon senior executives who ran the process for Bezos). *Rationale:* a primary-adjacent account (written by the people who operated the mechanism, not third-party observers) of the discipline Amazon uses to force strategic coherence *before* building: a mock press release plus external/internal FAQ, written from the customer's future vantage point, that a proposal must survive before it gets resourced — a structural technique for preventing strategy-free roadmap items from ever entering the pipeline.
- **John Cutler, North Star framework / strategy statements** — [cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-2853-words-before-numbers-north](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-2853-words-before-numbers-north). *Rationale:* Cutler (ex-Amplitude, widely read product strategy practitioner) argues the North Star *statement* — a 30–60 second, words-first articulation of the current strategy and its levers — must exist and be revisable *before* a North Star *metric* is chosen, because teams that jump straight to a number end up optimizing a proxy nobody agreed represents the strategy. This is the direct check for whether a "north star metric" on a dashboard is actually load-bearing or decorative.

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Take the top 10 items currently in progress or recently shipped: can each be connected via an explicit, named causal chain to the stated Challenge/Target Condition, or does the connection require inventing a justification after the fact?
- Is there a single, current, words-first strategy statement that a random contributor could recite approximately correctly — or does "the strategy" only exist as a slide from a planning offsite six months ago that nobody has revisited?
- For any item that *cannot* be traced to strategy: is it (a) genuine technical debt/foundation work explicitly exempted, (b) an opportunistic bet explicitly logged as a bet, or (c) simply undocumented drift?
- Would a Working-Backwards-style PR-FAQ for the current top initiative actually survive scrutiny — does the "customer" benefit hold up, or does the FAQ reveal the initiative is solving an internal/organizational problem dressed as a customer one?
- Is the north-star metric (if one exists) something the team can move by doing the *wrong* thing (gaming a proxy) — and if so, has anyone stress-tested that?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Roadmap-as-backlog:** a prioritized list of asks with no visible strategy layer above it — Perri's core diagnosis of the build trap, and the single most common failure this scope catches.
- **Stale or decorative north star:** a metric chosen once, displayed on a dashboard, and never revisited even as the actual growth levers (per Cutler) change — the number keeps moving but nobody can say *why* moving it matters anymore.
- **Strategy theater:** a strategy document that exists but was never operationalized into the Challenge/Target-Condition chain Perri describes, so it has no actual gate-keeping power over what gets built.
- **PR-FAQ (or equivalent) skipped for major bets:** committing significant roadmap capacity to an initiative without ever writing down, in customer language, why it matters — a strong predictor that the initiative is serving an internal narrative, not a customer or strategic outcome.

## 6. Gap Analysis

**Core question:** *Where, specifically, does the current product underserve a real, named job — and where is it wasting effort overserving a job nobody is asking to have done better?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Clayton Christensen et al., Jobs-to-be-Done — "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'"** — [hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done), HBR, Sept 2016 (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan). *Rationale:* the definitive statement of JTBD theory — "when we buy a product, we essentially 'hire' it to help us do a job... if it does the job well, we hire it again; if it does a crummy job, we 'fire' it" — reframing gap analysis away from "what features are missing" toward "what job, with what functional/social/emotional dimensions, is still poorly served."
- **Tony Ulwick, Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) and the Opportunity Algorithm** — [strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation/market-opportunity/](https://strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation/market-opportunity/), Ulwick's own consultancy (Strategyn) site; Ulwick is credited as the originator of the JTBD-adjacent ODI methodology. *Rationale:* ODI is the only framework in this space that turns "gap" into an actual *score*: for each desired outcome, `Opportunity = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)`, weighting importance twice as heavily as (lack of) satisfaction. Outcomes scoring high (roughly >15 of 20) are **underserved** — real growth opportunity; outcomes scoring low (<10) are **overserved** — a signal to *stop* investing there, potentially even a signal for disruption from a simpler competitor. This directly operationalizes the difference between a genuine capability gap and a gap nobody would pay to close.
- **Nielsen Norman Group, Journey Mapping** — [nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/). *Rationale:* NN/g is the standard applied-UX-research authority; journey mapping is "the entire point... to uncover gaps in the user experience... and then take action to optimize the experience," particularly at channel/tool handoffs where friction concentrates — the complementary, workflow-level (rather than outcome-statement-level) lens for gap analysis, especially relevant where a product is one step in a longer multi-tool workflow (as most dev-tools are).

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- For the top 10–20 customer-stated "jobs" in this space, where does the product score high-importance/low-satisfaction (Ulwick underserved — investable) versus low-importance/high-satisfaction (overserved — a candidate for simplification or de-investment)?
- Mapped end-to-end across the full user journey (not just inside the product's own UI/CLI surface): where do handoffs to adjacent tools or manual steps introduce friction, context loss, or abandonment?
- Is the current roadmap actually targeted at the highest-opportunity-score gaps, or at the loudest/most recent feature request (recency and volume bias substituting for a scored gap analysis)?
- Are there jobs the product *actively but silently* fails at — i.e., users have quietly built workarounds or use a competing tool for one specific job while otherwise using this product — that never surface as a "complaint" because users adapted instead of asking?
- Is any part of the current feature surface serving a job that's already overserved (satisfaction ≥ importance) — meaning further investment there has near-zero marginal opportunity, however tempting to polish?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Feature-request-list gap analysis:** treating the support inbox or GitHub issues as the gap analysis, which systematically overweights vocal users and underweights silent job failure (the user who just left, or who quietly built a shell script instead of filing an issue).
- **Ungraded gaps:** listing gaps without any importance/satisfaction scoring, so prioritization defaults to whoever argues loudest in the planning meeting rather than the Ulwick-style opportunity score.
- **In-product tunnel vision:** journey-mapping only the product's own screens/commands instead of the full task the user is actually doing, missing gaps that live at the boundary with adjacent tools — often the highest-friction point in a multi-tool workflow.
- **Confusing "gap we could fill" with "gap someone would pay/switch to have filled"** — JTBD and ODI both exist specifically to keep gap analysis anchored to outcomes customers value, not capabilities that are merely technically absent.

## 7. Adjacent Opportunities / Expansion

**Core question:** *What is the next-most-defensible expansion of scope from here — and is now, structurally, the right time to pursue it, or is expansion premature relative to the core?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **H. Igor Ansoff, "Strategies for Diversification"** — HBR, 1957, pp. 113–124; original scan: [archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr](https://archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr) (full text extraction was not machine-readable via automated fetch — the scan is an image-based PDF; the framework content below is confirmed via multiple secondary summaries and is uncontested in the strategy literature). *Rationale:* the original product-market growth matrix — **Market Penetration** (existing product, existing market — lowest risk), **Market Development** (existing product, new market), **Product Development** (new product, existing market), **Diversification** (new product, new market — highest risk, requiring both new customer *and* new product-execution risk simultaneously) — is the foundational vocabulary for classifying *any* expansion move by what kind of risk it actually adds.
- **Chris Dixon, "Come for the Tool, Stay for the Network"** — [cdixon.org/2015/01/31/come-for-the-tool-stay-for-the-network/](https://cdixon.org/2015/01/31/come-for-the-tool-stay-for-the-network/). *Rationale:* Dixon (general partner, a16z, prolific early-stage dev-tool/infra investor) names the specific expansion pattern most relevant to single-player developer tools: bootstrap adoption with a standalone tool that's useful even with zero other users, then grow a network/platform/ecosystem layer on top of the installed base once critical mass is reached — the tool gets you distribution, the network is where the durable value and defensibility (see Scope 9) accrues. Directly a Product-Development-into-Diversification move in Ansoff's terms, executed in a specific, well-tested sequence.
- **Bill Aulet, beachhead-to-adjacent-market sequencing (Disciplined Entrepreneurship)** — same source as Scope 3: [mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/disciplined-entrepreneurship-6-questions-startup-success](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/disciplined-entrepreneurship-6-questions-startup-search). *Rationale:* Aulet's explicit framing of the beachhead market as "a strategic base to expand to adjacent markets" gives a disciplined trigger condition for *when* to expand — after the beachhead is actually won and defensible, not before — directly countering premature expansion.

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Classify every expansion currently in motion or under discussion on the Ansoff grid: is it Market Penetration (deepen the core), Market Development (same product, new segment/vertical/platform), Product Development (new capability, same users), or Diversification (new capability, new users)? Diversification bets should be named as such and budgeted with proportionally higher risk tolerance and lower confidence, not funded like a Penetration bet.
- Has the beachhead (per Aulet) actually been won — is there real, measured dominance/dependency in the initial narrow segment — before capacity is diverted to an adjacent market or product line?
- Is there a credible "tool → network" path (per Dixon) available from the current single-player value proposition, and if so, what specifically is the trigger condition (installed base size, usage frequency, multi-user overlap) for pulling that lever, versus pulling it on founder enthusiasm alone?
- For platform/ecosystem adjacency specifically: does opening the product to third-party extension actually compound the core value (more use → more value for every user), or does it just add surface area and support burden without a network-effect payoff?
- What existing capability, asset, or data does the org have that a *new* entrant to the adjacent market would have to build from scratch — i.e., is the adjacency a genuine unfair-advantage move, or just "we could build that too"?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns — the risk of premature expansion

- **Diversification dressed as Penetration:** funding and staffing a genuinely new-product/new-market bet with the confidence and resourcing discipline appropriate to a low-risk core improvement, then being surprised when it underperforms — Ansoff's whole point is that these are categorically different risk profiles.
- **Expanding before the beachhead is won:** chasing an adjacent market while the core segment still has low retention, weak PMF (Scope 1), or open competitive vulnerability (Scope 2) — classic premature scaling, spreading a not-yet-defensible core thinner.
- **Building the network layer before the tool has distribution:** attempting Dixon's "network" stage without first earning the single-player "tool" adoption that was supposed to bootstrap it — network effects require a critical mass the tool stage was meant to generate.
- **Adjacency chosen for founder/team interest rather than unfair advantage:** expanding into a space that's merely *adjacent-sounding* (same buyer persona, unrelated capability) rather than one where the existing asset base (data, integration surface, installed base, trust) gives a structural head start a fresh competitor lacks.

## 8. Business Model / Monetization & Viability

**Core question:** *Even if the product is valuable, usable, and buildable, can the rest of the business (pricing, packaging, legal, GTM, and — for an open-source-adjacent product — the license/monetization boundary) actually capture and sustain that value?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Marty Cagan, "The Four Big Risks" (business viability risk)** — [svpg.com/four-big-risks/](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/) (direct fetch returned HTTP 403 during this research; content below is corroborated via multiple independent secondary summaries of Cagan's own published framework and is consistent with his book *INSPIRED*, 2nd ed., Wiley 2017). *Rationale:* Cagan (founder, Silicon Valley Product Group; formerly HP, Netscape, eBay product leadership) is the most widely cited voice in modern product management practice for the four-risk model: **Value** (will customers choose to use/buy it), **Usability** (can users figure out how to use it), **Feasibility** (can engineering build it with available time/skill/technology), and **Business Viability** (can legal, finance, sales, marketing, and executive stakeholders actually support it) — and his explicit later revision, moving away from a three-attribute "valuable/usable/feasible" model specifically because it let teams under-weight viability, is itself a documented lesson that business-model risk is the easiest risk category to skip.
- **Joseph Jacks (OSS Capital), the COSS (Commercial Open Source Software) definition and open-core pattern** — [medium.com/sand-hill-road/how-open-source-software-is-eating-software-with-joseph-jacks-from-oss-capital-ac98cc6669c3](https://medium.com/sand-hill-road/how-open-source-software-is-eating-software-with-joseph-jacks-from-oss-capital-ac98cc6669c3). *Rationale:* Jacks coined and formalized the COSS category and founded the first VC fund dedicated exclusively to it; his working definition — "if the company fundamentally exists because the open source project exists, the company is definitionally a COSS company" (examples given: MapR/Hortonworks/Cloudera without Hadoop, GitHub/GitLab without Git, Red Hat without Linux, HashiCorp without Vagrant) — is the sharpest available test for whether an OSS-adjacent product's business model is structurally coherent with its open-source core, independent of which specific monetization pattern (open-core, hosted/managed service, dual-license, support/services) is chosen. The **open-core** pattern itself — free core, proprietary/paid layer on top — is the most common instance; term-origin note (tertiary, historical-attribution only): coined by Andrew Lampitt in 2008 per [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-core_model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-core_model).

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Applying Cagan: has business-viability risk been assessed with the same rigor as value/usability/feasibility, or has the team (per Cagan's own confession about his earlier framework) quietly treated "we built something valuable and usable" as sufficient and assumed the business model would sort itself out?
- Specifically for viability: does legal (licensing, IP, export/compliance), finance (unit economics, CAC/LTV if applicable), sales/marketing (can this actually be sold or adopted through the intended channel), and leadership (does it fit the stated company strategy) each sign off, or is viability assumed by default?
- Applying the COSS test: if the product has an open-source core, would the company (or the initiative, if internal) *cease to have a reason to exist* without that open-source project — and does the monetization boundary (what's free vs. paid) track a real value line, or does it feel arbitrary/bait-and-switch to the community?
- Pricing/packaging: is the unit of value charged for (seats, usage, outcome, a proprietary layer) the same unit the customer perceives value in, or is there a mismatch that creates friction or resentment at the point of payment?
- For an OSS-adjacent dev tool specifically: what is the plan if a well-resourced incumbent (a platform vendor, an IDE maker, a model provider) ships the open-source core's functionality natively for free — does the paid layer still hold value, or does it evaporate?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Viability as an afterthought:** the exact trap Cagan documents in his own framework's revision history — teams that are rigorous about value/usability/feasibility and treat "can the business actually sustain this" as someone else's problem, discovered late.
- **Open-core boundary drawn for revenue convenience, not value coherence:** paywalling something the community expected to be free (the classic "bait-and-switch" perception risk explicitly named in open-core criticism) erodes the trust that made the open-source distribution strategy work in the first place.
- **No COSS test applied:** building "open source, but monetized" without ever checking whether the company's existence is actually load-bearing on the open-source project (Jacks's test) — if not, the OSS positioning is marketing, not a business model, and will be treated as such by sophisticated buyers/contributors.
- **Pricing the wrong unit:** charging for a proxy (seats) when the value delivered scales with a different unit (usage, outcome, data processed) — a common source of both revenue leakage and customer resentment.

## 9. Moat / Defensibility

**Core question:** *If a well-funded competitor copied every visible feature tomorrow, what would still be true only of this product — and is that "still true" thing actually durable, or does it erode as soon as it's named?*

### Authoritative framework(s)

- **Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy*** — official site/synopsis: [7powers.com/synopsis/](https://7powers.com/synopsis/); detailed secondary summary verified by fetch: [blas.com/7-powers/](https://blas.com/7-powers/). *Rationale:* Helmer (Stanford strategy lecturer, longtime investor) built the most rigorous modern taxonomy of what actually counts as a durable competitive advantage. Verified core definitions: a "Power" requires *both* a **Benefit** (materially improves cash flow — higher prices, lower costs, or lower required investment) and a **Barrier** (something that stops competitors from arbitraging the benefit away) — a mechanism can be a real, current advantage and still not be a Power if it lacks a barrier. The seven: **Scale Economies** (declining unit cost with size), **Network Economies** (value rises with more users), **Counter-Positioning** (a new business model incumbents won't copy because copying would damage their existing business), **Switching Costs** (value from consistency across a customer's purchases over time, making leaving costly), **Branding** (accumulated trust/willingness-to-pay that takes years to replicate), **Cornered Resource** (preferential access to a scarce asset, by "fiat" not competition), and **Process Power** (operational excellence baked into tacit, hard-to-copy organizational routines, compounded over time — "hysteresis"). Framework equation: `Value = Market Size × Power`; a Power must be **Superior** (improves cash flow), **Significant** (materially), and **Sustainable** (largely immune to competitive arbitrage).
- **NFX, "The Network Effects Manual"** — [nfx.com/post/network-effects-manual](https://www.nfx.com/post/network-effects-manual). *Rationale:* NFX (a network-effects-focused VC firm) built the most granular public taxonomy of *which kind* of network effect a product actually has — verified by fetch: 16 named types across categories from strongest/hardest-to-replicate (Physical, Protocol) through two-sided (Marketplace, Platform) to weakest (Language, Bandwagon) — critical because "network effects" is frequently claimed as a moat when what actually exists is a much weaker social/bandwagon dynamic that competitors can erode quickly.
- **Ben Thompson, "Aggregation Theory" and "The Moat Map"** — [stratechery.com/aggregation-theory/](https://stratechery.com/aggregation-theory/); [stratechery.com/2018/the-moat-map/](https://stratechery.com/2018/the-moat-map/). *Rationale:* Thompson (Stratechery) is the standard modern voice on how zero-marginal-distribution-cost internet businesses build moats not by owning supply but by owning the *demand* relationship/user choice architecture — verified by fetch: "old moats are gone — and new ones can be built — and Aggregation Theory helps you identify both," with the Moat Map framing defensibility as the interaction of supplier differentiation and network effects on the demand side. Directly relevant to any product whose moat thesis rests on being the trusted intermediary/interface layer rather than on owning a proprietary underlying capability.

### Concrete audit questions this scope forces

- Run the Helmer test on every claimed moat: does it have *both* a Benefit and a Barrier? A "benefit with no barrier" (e.g., "we're faster/better right now") is a **current advantage**, not a Power, and will erode the moment a competitor decides to close the gap.
- If network effects are claimed, which of the 16 NFX types specifically — and is it a strong type (Protocol, Platform) or a weak one (Bandwagon, Language) that a competitor with a marketing budget could substantially neutralize?
- Switching costs: are they *earned* (genuine integration depth, accumulated configuration/data/history the user would have to redo) or *imposed* (lock-in via friction/opacity) — the former compounds goodwill, the latter invites churn the moment a credible alternative appears and actively damages the moat's other pillar (branding/trust).
- Counter-positioning: is there a business-model or workflow choice competitors structurally *can't* copy without damaging their own existing business — or is the current advantage just a head start that any well-resourced entrant could replicate without cost?
- Applying the "tools vs. brain" moat thesis specifically (relevant to any AI-agent-infrastructure product): a *tool* (a CLI, a search index, a code-mapping utility) is close to Helmer's "benefit with no barrier" by default — the underlying technique is frequently public, documentable, and reimplementable by a competent team in weeks. Ask explicitly: is the durable asset actually the tool, or is it (a) a **Cornered Resource** — proprietary data/telemetry/traces the tool alone generates and accumulates, (b) a **Process Power** — an orchestration/judgment layer whose quality compounds with usage in a way that's hard to replicate quickly even with the same tools, or (c) neither, in which case the "brain not tools" claim is a hope, not yet a Power, and should be tested against Helmer's Superior/Significant/Sustainable bar rather than asserted.
- Aggregation/Thompson lens: does the product own the *demand-side relationship* (the agent/developer trusts and routes through it by default) in a way that compounds as more suppliers/tools plug in underneath — or is it itself a replaceable supplier plugged into someone else's aggregation layer (e.g., the IDE, the agent runtime, the model provider)?

### Common failure modes / anti-patterns

- **Calling a current advantage a moat:** the single most common error per Helmer's own framework — "we're ahead right now" is not a Power without an identified Barrier that survives a competitor's best copying effort.
- **Overclaiming network effects:** labeling ordinary user-growth or word-of-mouth as a "network effect" when, per NFX's taxonomy, no user's experience actually improves as more users join — this is growth, not defensibility.
- **Moat-by-assertion in AI-agent tooling specifically:** claiming "the orchestration/brain is the moat, the tools are commodities" without naming which Helmer power the brain actually has — if the brain's advantage is just "we thought of the right prompt/workflow first," that has no barrier and is not sustainable; the claim only holds if there's a genuine cornered resource (proprietary interaction data feeding back into quality) or process power (compounding organizational learning) underneath it.
- **Confusing switching costs with lock-in resentment:** building deliberate friction to exit rather than deliberate value-to-stay — the former is fragile (a single bad PR moment or a credible open migration path erodes it fast) and actively undermines the Branding power, since trust and imposed lock-in are in tension.
- **Ignoring the Value = Market Size × Power equation:** a genuine, well-defended Power in a tiny market is still a small business — defensibility analysis without a market-size gate (Scope 3) can lead to over-investing in protecting a moat around a market too small to matter.
